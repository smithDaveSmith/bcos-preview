The rule people miss

Two rules run at the same time. Only one binds.

Most directors know the 56-day rule. Fewer know there is a second calculation running alongside it, and that for many companies it produces the earlier date — the one that actually binds.

Rule one

Annual return date
+ 56 days

The one everyone has in the diary.

Rule two

Financial year end
+ 9 months + 56 days

Applies wherever financial statements are attached — which is almost every company.

Which one applies

Whichever falls earlier

For a company whose year end sits well in front of its annual return date, rule two bites first — and it is routinely missed.

What it costs to be late

€100 the day after. Then €3 a day, to €0.

The penalty is mechanical. It does not care why the return was late, and it accrues every day until it is filed.

At day 1 the accrued penalty is €100

The €20 filing fee is separate. Figures from the CRO.

And beyond the penalty

01

Late filing penalty

€100 the day after the deadline, then €3 per day, capped at €1,200 per return.

02

Loss of audit exemption

Lost where a company files late more than once in a five-year period.

03

Prosecution of the company and its directors

Failure to file is an offence under section 871 of the Companies Act 2014, with a Class A fine of up to €5,000 on summary conviction. The CRO may also impose an on-the-spot fine for persistent late filing.

04

Involuntary strike-off

The Registrar may begin strike-off proceedings. Once dissolved, company assets vest in the Minister for Public Expenditure and directors may be personally liable for debts incurred while the company stands dissolved.

Under section 22 of the Companies (Corporate Governance, Enforcement and Regulatory Provisions) Act 2024, a company loses the audit exemption if it files late more than once in a five-year period. This replaced the earlier position, under which a single late filing cost the exemption for the following two years.

The statutory calendar

A company's first year

incorporation

Day 0

Incorporated

The certificate issues. Two statutory clocks start on this date — not on the day you begin trading.

Month 5

Beneficial ownership due

A new company has five months to record its ultimate beneficial owners at the RBO. Any later change must be filed within 14 days.

Month 6

First annual return due

The first annual return falls due six months after incorporation. No financial statements are required with it.

Month 9

Financial year end

From here the second rule starts running: year end plus nine months plus 56 days. For most companies this is the one that binds.

Deadline

The filing deadline

Whichever of the two rules falls earlier. Miss it and the penalty begins the following day.

And after

€100, then €3 a day

The penalty is mechanical, running to a maximum of €1,200 per return. Audit exemption is lost on a second late filing within five years.

Starting one

Four company types. Pick the one that fits the structure.

The type you incorporate determines how many directors you need, whether the secretary can also be a director, and what the members are exposed to. It is worth getting right at the start.

LTD

Private Company Limited by Shares

The most common company type for small and medium enterprises.

Directors
Minimum of one director
Secretary
The company secretary cannot also be the sole director
Liability
The shareholders have limited liability

Requirements →

A private company limited by shares, incorporated to carry out a specific activity.

What changed, and when.

Company law moves. A note written while something was still a Bill is worth re-reading once it is an Act.

Correction Reviewed October 2026

A single late filing no longer costs you the audit exemption

Under section 22 of the Companies (Corporate Governance, Enforcement and Regulatory Provisions) Act 2024, audit exemption is lost where a company files late more than once in a five-year period — not on a single late filing.

The rule people miss Reviewed October 2026

The European Union (Adjustments of Size Criteria for Certain Companies and Groups) Regulations 2024 raised the balance sheet and turnover thresholds for micro, small, medium and large companies by 25%.
